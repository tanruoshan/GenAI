\subsection{Task and Data}
Given a sentence whose damaged span is known and whose garbled letters are visible, the task is to predict one word
per damaged position; both repair methods take the same input and are scored by the same code (Sec.~\ref{sec:slot}).
We build this task on \bln{} \citep{booth-etal-2024-bln600}, 600 excerpts of London crime reporting from 1830--1890s
British newspapers (572 from three related weeklies), each with its source image, OCR output and a gold
transcription, released under CC BY-NC-ND. We split excerpts into sentences with \texttt{pysbd} and pool sentences of
20--60 words with at least two \emph{fact tokens}: a number, any word containing a digit or pound sign, or a
capitalised word that is not the first word of the sentence (a short stop list excludes titles such as Mr, Mrs, The).
This is a surface rule, not a named-entity tagger: it catches "Old" in "Old Bailey" and misses a lower-cased fact, and
it flags dates only through capitalisation or digits, not through date parsing. The pool holds 3,123 sentences from
592 excerpts. From it we draw, with a fixed seed, 150 test and 20 dev sentences from 157 excerpts (at most three per
excerpt, no excerpt in both splits, balanced across three sentence-length bands); dev is used only for tuning and is
never scored as a result.

\subsection{Damage Simulation}
\label{sec:damage}
For each sentence we draw one anchor among its fact tokens -- always damaged, never dropped -- and damage one
contiguous span of $k$ words around it, with $k$ set so the span covers 1 word, then 10, 25, 50 or 75\% of the
sentence (half-up rounding); spans are nested, so the same place in the sentence gets worse from level to level
rather than moving around. Each damaged word is dropped outright with probability 0.10 (never the anchor); a
surviving word has 20--30\% of its letters and digits altered, about 15\% of those by deletion (at most two per
word) and the rest by substitution, drawn from a confusion table calibrated on 29 held-out \bln{} excerpts (85.7\% of
substitutions), a capitalised table entry (3.6\%), a look-alike map such as O/0 or rn/m (4.9\%), or a random
same-kind character (5.8\%); punctuation is never touched. The 20--30\% band was chosen to sit around the median
per-word character error rate we measured on those held-out excerpts (0.25, IQR 0.17--0.40); an earlier 40--60\%
setting sat above that range and was dropped. This calibration comes from mild, real OCR errors on aligned words, so
it models the kind of noise OCR output already contains, not a direct simulation of a torn or stained physical page
(the Limitations section returns to this). Table~\ref{tab:damage} shows the result on the test split: sentence-level
CER grows roughly in step with real \bln{} OCR error rates, but the share of damaged words that are themselves facts
falls from 100\% at the 1-word level to 17\% at 75\%, so "fact recovery" is not measuring the same mixture of words
at every level. About one in ten fact slots is dropped outright, leaving no visible letters for that fact at all.

\subsection{Shared Slot Input}
\label{sec:slot}
Both methods see the same \emph{slot view}: the sentence's undamaged context is left untouched, and each damaged
gold word is replaced by a slot showing its garbled form (a dropped word becomes an empty slot, written $\langle
?\rangle$). This view is built from the gold tokens and the per-word edit operations, never from the raw corrupted
text, so it cannot leak an easier version of the task. A predicted word is spliced back inside the original word's
edge punctuation, so context words are never rewritten and every difference in score comes from what a method puts
in the slots. For example, the damaged sentence "A POWERFULLY-BUILT man, $\langle$same$\rangle$
$\langle$Bbonias$\rangle$ $\langle$Huicl,$\rangle$ $\langle$e$\rangle$ commission agent, \ldots{}" has the gold
continuation "named Thomas Hurd, a"; the garbled forms are visible to both repair methods, but the gold words are
not. Showing garbled rather than blanked letters keeps this an OCR-repair task rather than blind text infilling: an
OCR engine already flags such regions as low-confidence, and a reader of a damaged page still sees partial letters.

\subsection{Repair Methods}
\label{sec:models}
\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{(a) BERT + reranking.} We fine-tune \texttt{bert-base-cased} \citep{devlin2019bert} (110M parameters; cased
because fact tokens are partly defined by capitalisation) on clean gold text from the 443 excerpts held outside the
sample (2,939 sentences; a further 10\% of excerpts held out for validation, with zero excerpt or sentence overlap
with test or dev, which we verified directly). Training mirrors inference: for each target word, span words before it
are left as gold text, the target is masked with its true number of subword pieces, and later span words each become
one \texttt{[MASK]}. Fine-tuning uses a learning rate of $5\mathrm{e}{-5}$, batch size 16, up to 10 epochs with early
stopping (patience 2) after a 10\%-of-one-epoch warmup and linear decay; the best checkpoint (epoch 2) lowered the
per-piece validation loss from 5.61 (pretrained) to 4.14. At inference, slots are filled left to right; for the
current slot the model tries 1, 2 and 3 masks, keeps a beam of 5 pieces per position, and retains the top 10
candidates per mask count. A slot with a visible internal hyphen or apostrophe is split into sub-slots filled the
same way; only 0.9\% of slots (3.5\% of fact slots) still need more than three pieces. Each candidate is scored as its mean log-probability per piece plus $\lambda(1-d)$, where $d$ is the normalised
Levenshtein distance to the garbled form, so a fluent completion is pulled back toward what the damaged letters
actually show -- a direct, modern instance of
"context beats confusion" \citep{evershed2014correcting}. We chose $\lambda = 8$ from a grid over $\{1,2,4,8,16\}$ on
dev, where it gave the best exact-word rate at a point where Fact Recovery Rate had already plateaued. Dropped slots,
which have no garbled letters to match against, are scored on the language-model term alone.
\end{sloppypar}

\textbf{(b) LLM few-shot.} The LLM condition prompts an open-weight model with a system message, a task instruction,
three solved dev examples (held out from all scoring), and the same slot view as BERT, asking for a JSON list of
exactly $k$ strings at temperature 0. \emph{This track was not frozen at the time of writing}: development runs used
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \citep{grattafiori2024llama3} served through GWDG's SAIA API, with Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct as an
allowed alternative, but the final model, host and prompt version are being decided by a teammate on separate
hardware before the test split is scored -- which is also why the Introduction and Related Work name only "an
open-weight LLM" rather than a specific model. Only open-weight models hosted this way are used, since \bln{}'s
CC BY-NC-ND licence rules out sending it to a closed third-party API. The system message itself was necessary: without
it, an early check found the model answering in Python or tool-call syntax in 15 of 15 cases rather than plain JSON.
An answer of the wrong length, not valid JSON, or cut off by the token limit counts as a \emph{format failure} and is
scored as if no repair had been made, with no retry and no manual correction. A secondary variant supplies the whole
surrounding article as context instead of just the sentence (its other sentences are gold, not damaged, so this is an
upper bound rather than a fair comparison with BERT); we compare it only against the LLM's own few-shot run.
Zero-shot prompting was tried on dev and dropped, so this design cannot isolate how much of the LLM's performance
comes from the three examples themselves rather than the instruction alone.

\textbf{(c) No-repair baseline.} The damaged text itself is scored as if it were a third method, giving the floor
both repair methods have to beat. Table~\ref{tab:settings} summarises both methods' settings; the LLM row's model
field is left as \todo{final id} until Sec.~\ref{sec:models}(b)'s open point above is resolved.
