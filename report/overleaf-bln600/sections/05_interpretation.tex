\section{Interpretation and Conclusion}
\label{sec:interpretation}
% All numbers from the TEST run of notebook 4 (Sec. 4.3). Written 2026-09-29 after that run.

% ---- P1: answer the RQ and SQ1 directly ----
The objective was to find how far text repair can be trusted as the damaged share of a sentence grows. For the
letter-based methods the answer is a ceiling rather than a breakpoint: they are about as reliable at 75\% damage as
at one damaged word, but at no level better than about one fact in two (anchor 58\% to 53\% for BERT +
dictionary). The LLM loses facts as the damage grows (49\% to 25\%), most sharply between 25\% and 50\%, where its
format failures begin. The objective is reached only in
part. \textbf{H1} is partly supported (descriptively): quality falls with damage, but not faster in \frr{} than in
BERTScore (BERT + dictionary: \frr{} 0.58 to 0.48, exact words 0.58 to 0.57, span BERTScore 0.77 to 0.40). \textbf{H2} is not supported for the tested pair: the LLM never restores more
facts than BERT + dictionary, and significantly fewer at 50\% and 75\%; only BERT without dictionary candidates
restores fewer facts than the LLM. \textbf{H2b} is not supported for the tested pair: the few-shot LLM does not beat
the lookup on facts at any level and loses to it at 50\% and 75\%. \textbf{H3} is supported beyond one word, where
BERTScore ranks a wrong-fact repair at least as high as a right-fact repair in 11\% to 45\% of pairs; at one word
the span is the fact itself, so it cannot hold there.

% ---- P2: SQ2, which method and why ----
Format failures explain much of the LLM's decline at high damage (44\% of its answers at 75\%), but on facts even its
valid answers stay behind. Two differences can
explain this. First, the methods use the garbled letters differently: BERT sees them only through its candidates and
reranking term, the lookup uses them alone, and the LLM and BART read all garbled forms at once. We expected this
joint view to favour the LLM as spans grow; it did not. Instead, the methods that rank candidates by their letters lead: BERT with dictionary candidates (and its higher
letter weight) restores 0.33 to 0.44 more of the facts than BERT without them, but only 0.01 to 0.07 more than the
lookup alone. For a fact word with only some letters altered, the closest spelling in a word list is a strong guess
that a generative model does not reliably beat. Second, supervision and scale differ: BERT is
fine-tuned on in-domain text and BART on our own damage, while the LLM has about 270 times more parameters than BERT
(27 times more active per token) but sees five examples. A related post-OCR comparison likewise found fine-tuned
sequence-to-sequence models to correct more errors than generative LLMs, even fine-tuned ones \citep{debaene2025evaluating}. The probe found no near-verbatim continuation in 116 test sentences, which does not exclude other forms of memorisation.

% ---- P3: SQ3, meaning vs facts ----
Semantic similarity overstates how much factual content a repair restores. BART, trained on the test noise process
itself, scores highest among the main methods on span BERTScore from 10\% damage on while restoring fewer facts than BERT + dictionary, with
repairs such as \emph{Alexander Fleming} for \emph{Alexander Temple}: fluent, plausible and wrong. This matches
findings from summarisation and translation \citep{maynez2020faithfulness,hanna2021finegrained} and carries over to
text repair, even with BERTScore computed on the repaired span only.

% ---- P4: generality and use for archives ----
In practice, a restored name, place or number should not be trusted without a check at any damage level. How repair
performs on real damaged pages, with heavier noise and no known damaged region, remains untested. Archives should keep repaired words marked rather than replace damaged text silently, as
Ithaca pairs model predictions with historians' judgement \citep{assael2022restoring}.

% ---- P5: conclusion and future work ----
In short, when the damaged words are known and their letters visible, the best repair restores about half of the
fact words at every damage level; the letters carry most of this, and neither a few-shot LLM nor a fine-tuned
denoiser restores more facts than a dictionary lookup guided by BERT. Three extensions follow from our assumptions:
combining repair with detection of the damaged region; testing on real OCR errors from damaged pages; and repairing
from the scanned image itself, through image-level degradation as a test bed \citep{hamdi2020assessing} or
vision-capable models that read the damaged page directly \citep[cf.][]{kanerva2025ocr}.
