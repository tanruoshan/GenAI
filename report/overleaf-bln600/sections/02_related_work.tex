\paragraph{SQ1: grading damage for repair.} We know of no study that controls damage severity for the repair step
itself; graded severity has so far been used only for \emph{reading} OCR'd text. Post-OCR research splits the task
into error \emph{detection} and error \emph{correction} \citep{nguyen2021survey}, scored separately in the ICDAR 2019
competition \citep{rigaud2019icdar}; we study correction only and assume the damaged span is known. Results on how
well GenAI corrects disagree. An instruction-tuned Llama~2 cut CER on BLN600 further than a fine-tuned BART
\citep{thomas2024leveraging}, while 14 prompted LLMs mostly degraded historical transcripts
\citep{boros2024postcorrection}; on early modern Dutch, fine-tuned seq2seq models beat fine-tuned generative ones
\citep{debaene2025evaluating}; and prompted open-weight LLMs lowered CER for English but not for Finnish
\citep{kanerva2025ocr}. These results diverge by training regime, language and source quality, but in all of them
the amount of damage is whatever the OCR produced: it can be observed, not set, so they cannot show at what point
repair stops working. Controlled severity levels do exist for reading OCR text: page images degraded at two levels
per degradation type and then OCR'd have been used to measure named-entity recognition \citep{hamdi2020assessing},
and real OCR output has been binned into quality bands for other downstream tasks \citep{vanstrien2020assessing}.
SQ1 applies this grading logic to the repair step.

\paragraph{SQ2: restoring text at known gaps.} The closest prior task is restoring ancient text at known positions.
Pythia predicts 1--10 hidden characters in Greek inscriptions \citep{assael2019restoring}, Ithaca extends this and
pairs predictions with historians' judgement \citep{assael2022restoring}, and a masked language model fills gaps in
Akkadian texts, taking the number of missing signs as given and extending token predictions to multi-sign gaps with
a beam search, with accuracy falling as the gap grows \citep{lazar2021filling}. These studies differ from ours in
three ways. They hide characters completely, whereas OCR damage leaves letters garbled but partly readable, which our
slot input exploits (Sec.~\ref{sec:slot}); their gaps are at most a few tokens, while ours reach 75\% of a sentence
(SQ1); and none compares a masked language model with a prompted LLM (SQ2) or scores facts apart from overall
similarity (SQ3). On the model side, BERT fills masked positions in parallel \citep{devlin2019bert}, which makes
multi-token blanks harder to fill \citep{shen2020blank}; our search over candidate lengths
(Sec.~\ref{sec:models}) follows the beam decoding of \citet{lazar2021filling}. Left-to-right infilling
\citep{donahue2020enabling} is the natural counterpart for a prompted LLM, and our reranking adopts the classic OCR
idea of weighing context against letter evidence \citep{evershed2014correcting}. Filling a blank from context is
generation conditioned on the rest of the sentence \citep{donahue2020enabling}, and BERT can be sampled to produce
text \citep{wang2019bert}, so both methods count as GenAI here.\footnote{We cite \citet{wang2019bert} only for this
narrow claim; its Markov-random-field derivation was later shown to be incorrect by one of its authors. Read
sceptically, BERT is the fine-tuned in-domain baseline the few-shot LLM must beat, which is how our experiment treats
it either way.}

\paragraph{SQ3: meaning versus facts.} A similarity score can pass a repair that gets the facts wrong, and SQ3 tests
whether that happens here. BERTScore \citep{zhang2020bertscore} is the standard similarity metric for generated text,
but in summarisation it correlates only weakly with human judgements of faithfulness (Spearman 0.19) and factuality
(0.12) \citep{maynez2020faithfulness}, and in machine translation it fails to penalise a wrong candidate that shares
most content words with the reference \citep{hanna2021finegrained}, in part because BERT represents names and
numbers poorly (as reviewed ibid.). A repaired span that restores every word but one name is exactly such a
candidate. Since this evidence comes from other tasks, SQ3 checks it directly by scoring the same repairs with
BERTScore and with the Fact Recovery Rate. A second concern is memorisation. \citet{thomas2024leveraging} chose
BLN600 over the ICDAR benchmarks to avoid contamination, but BLN600 itself has been public since 2024, so a newer LLM
may have seen it in pretraining \citep{sainz2023contamination}. We probe for this with a simplified form of guided completion
\citep{golchin2024time}, prompting the model with the start of a test sentence and comparing its continuation with
the true remainder.

No single element of our design is new; the combination is. Table~\ref{tab:position} places our study against the
three closest lines of work, and Section~\ref{sec:method} builds each missing element: graded nested damage
(Sec.~\ref{sec:damage}), a shared slot input (Sec.~\ref{sec:slot}), two repair families and a non-GenAI lookup
under identical input (Sec.~\ref{sec:models}), and a fact-level score next to BERTScore (Sec.~\ref{sec:metrics}).
